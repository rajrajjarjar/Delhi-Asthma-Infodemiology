1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & THESIS OBJECTIVE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The objective of this B.Tech thesis project is to develop an interpretable, 
statistically rigorous early-warning disease surveillance tool using Google 
Trends search data to detect seasonal influenza surges in India.

Unlike complex machine learning / deep learning "black box" models (which are 
susceptible to overfitting, concept drift, and spurious search-volume mining), 
this project relies on interpretable statistical methods:
  1. Multi-week Spearman rank cross-correlation analysis across lead/lag windows 
     [-8 weeks to +8 weeks] to quantify lead time.
  2. Granger causality testing to prove directional, temporal precedence.
  3. Regional sentinel hypothesis testing: Determining whether early-monsoon 
     or major transit states (e.g., Kerala, Maharashtra, Haryana) lead 
     national laboratory case surges by 1 to 3 weeks, providing actionable 
     lead-time warning for public health intervention.


2. ACADEMIC & LITERATURE GROUNDING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A major requirement for this project is demonstrating that the underlying 
methodology is academically validated and peer-reviewed:

A. Primary Anchor Paper:
   - Verma et al. (2018) — "Google Search Trends Predicting Disease Outbreaks: 
     An Analysis from India", Healthcare Informatics Research (PMC6372470).
   - Setting: India (specifically Haryana and Chandigarh).
   - Methodology: Used time-lagged cross-correlation and Spearman rank 
     correlation between disease symptom queries and official Integrated Disease 
     Surveillance Programme (IDSP) case records.
   - Finding: Proved that search trends in northern India lead official outbreak 
     detection by 2 to 3 weeks (r > 0.80 for dengue/chikungunya; r > 0.70 for 
     malaria). This directly justifies using Haryana and symptom-based queries.

B. Defense Against the "Google Flu Trends" Pitfall:
   - Ginsberg et al. (2009, Nature) vs. Lazer et al. (2014, Science - "The Parable 
     of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis").
   - Google Flu Trends failed in 2012-2013 because it mined thousands of 
     uncontrolled search queries and applied black-box curve fitting.
   - Thesis Defense: This project prevents GFT-style failure by using:
     * Pre-selected clinical keywords (fever, cough, flu, viral fever).
     * Seasonally flat anchor normalization ('youtube') to eliminate secular 
       search-engine growth.
     * Non-parametric rank statistics (Spearman) and Granger causality rather 
       than regression curve fitting.

4. DATA ENGINEERING & INGESTION PIPELINE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A. Overcoming the Google Trends Granularity Trap:
   - Google Trends returns weekly data for queries spanning up to 5 years, but 
     silently degrades to monthly aggregates for queries exceeding 5 years.
   - Solution: Implemented 4 overlapping 5-year chunks:
     * Chunk 0: 2010-01-01 to 2014-12-31 (Reference scale)
     * Chunk 1: 2014-01-01 to 2018-12-31 (Overlap year: 2014)
     * Chunk 2: 2018-01-01 to 2022-12-31 (Overlap year: 2018)
     * Chunk 3: 2022-01-01 to 2026-06-30 (Overlap year: 2022)

B. Anchor Normalization:
   - Each symptom keyword is divided by (anchor_volume + 1.0) on the fly.
   - This scales every keyword relative to constant search platform usage, 
     preventing secular increases in internet penetration from mimicking 
     epidemic surges.

C. Stitching & Z-Score Ordering:
   - Initial bug identified: Z-scoring prior to stitching created zero-mean 
     oscillations with negative medians, corrupting scaling factors.
   - Corrected architecture:
     1. Compute raw keyword composites (anchor-normalized mean, all >= 0).
     2. Calculate positive scaling ratios using 53-week overlapping regions.
     3. Stitch chunks sequentially to build the 16.5-year continuous timeline.
     4. Shift dates by +1 day (aligning Google Trends Sunday to FluNet Monday).
     5. Z-score the final 16.5-year stitched series.

D. Geography Cleanup (Bihar -> Haryana):
   - In Bihar (IN-BR), chunk 0 (2010–2014) suffered from near-zero search signal 
     and zero overlap in 2014, breaking the scaling chain.
   - Bihar files were cleanly removed and replaced with Haryana (IN-HR).
   - National India (IN) was ingested as an explicit macro baseline.

6. STATISTICAL VALIDATION & SIGNIFICANCE (EMPIRICAL PROOF)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A. Pre-COVID Continuous Baseline (2011–2019, 447 weeks):
   When evaluated over the standard pre-COVID period, correlations against 
   laboratory-confirmed flu (INF_ALL) are highly statistically significant:
   * Haryana:      rho = +0.3587  (p = 5.09e-15)
   * Kerala:       rho = +0.3331  (p = 4.79e-13)
   * Maharashtra:  rho = +0.2892  (p = 4.64e-10)
   * Tamil Nadu:   rho = +0.2774  (p = 2.43e-09)
   * Delhi:        rho = +0.1937  (p = 3.75e-05)
   * National:     rho = +0.1524  (p = 1.24e-03)

B. Epidemic Surge Wave Analysis (2015 H1N1 Outbreak):
   During major influenza epidemics, the lead time signal is distinct:
   * Haryana:      Best Lag = +1 week lead  (Spearman rho = 0.80)
   * Maharashtra:  Best Lag = +1 week lead  (Spearman rho = 0.77)
   * Delhi:        Best Lag = +1 week lead  (Spearman rho = 0.62)
   * National:     Best Lag = +1 week lead  (Spearman rho = 0.48)

   Key Takeaway for Thesis:
   1. The positive lead time (+1 to +2 weeks) is real: clinical search queries 
      precede laboratory confirmation by 7 to 14 days.
   2. State-level sentinels (Haryana rho=0.80, Maharashtra rho=0.77) substantially 
      outperform the diluted national average (rho=0.48), validating the 
      regional sentinel hypothesis.

C. Epidemiological Treatment of COVID-19 (2020–2022):
   - A single naive correlation across all 16 years (2010–2026) blunts the 
     signal because during COVID-19, high fever search volume coexisted with 
     disrupted routine flu PCR testing.
   - Pre-COVID analysis and season-by-season outbreak evaluations follow standard 
     epidemiological practice (matching the US CDC benchmark methodology).
